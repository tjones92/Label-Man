Three albums that each attack a different axis. Aretha breaks the model in a new place — and it's the one gap we haven't touched in five rounds.

## I Never Loved a Man the Way I Love You (Atlantic, March 1967)

|#|Track|Archetype|Lyric mode|Vocal approach|Mood|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|1|Respect|Horn-driven soul number|**Demand / boast**|Belted + call-and-response|Commanding|
|2|Drown in My Own Tears|Deep soul pleader|Confessional|Testifying|Desolate|
|3|I Never Loved a Man|Deep soul pleader|Confessional|Testifying|Helpless|
|4|Soul Serenade|Groove / riff vamp|Romantic address|Conversational|Languid|
|5|Don't Let Me Lose This Dream|Charm song _(3/4)_|Romantic address|Conversational|Hopeful|
|6|Baby, Baby, Baby|Deep soul pleader _(6/8)_|Romantic address|Testifying|Yearning|
|7|Dr. Feelgood|Slow blues _(TwelveBar)_|**Demand / boast**|Testifying|Possessive|
|8|Good Times|Shuffle / twelve-bar|Advice / exhortation|Conversational|Easy|
|9|Do Right Woman|Deep soul pleader|Advice / exhortation|Belted|Admonishing|
|10|Save Me|Horn-driven soul number|Romantic address|Shouted|Frantic|
|11|A Change Is Gonna Come|Dramatic ballad / big build|Topical / testimony|Testifying|Resolute|

**The missing axis: Vocal approach.** Four of these are Deep soul pleaders in the same tempo band, and the archetype column flattens them — but nobody experiences this album as repetitive, because the differentiation is entirely in _delivery_. Right now two singers could take identical Archetype + Form + Genre + Production and the model couldn't tell a crooner from a shouter. That's a big hole in a game where artists are the asset.

`VocalApproach: Crooned · Conversational · Belted · Testifying/melismatic · Shouted · Harmony-blend · Spoken · Deadpan`

Make it an **artist stat with a range**, not just a track tag. A Sinatra has Crooned/Conversational and can't reach Testifying; Aretha has the widest range on the board. It's the single most scoutable thing about a singer and you currently can't see it.

**Respect is the best argument in the project for archetype-on-recording.** Otis's 1965 original and Aretha's 1967 cover share composition, archetype (Horn-driven soul number), tempo band and genre. What changed: Lyric mode flipped from plea to **Demand**, Vocal approach went Shouted → Belted with call-and-response, and the arrangement added the spelled hook and "sock it to me" (Carolyn and Erma Franklin, neither credited as writer). Same song, 14× the cultural impact. If your cover system re-rolls those fields while the publishing flows back to Redding's estate, that one example teaches players the entire mechanic.

**New lyric mode: Demand / boast.** Second-person assertion from strength — not counsel (Advice), not supplication (Romantic address). _Respect_, _Dr. Feelgood_, _Hoochie Coochie Man_, _I'm a Man_, _Think_. It's the backbone of blues and the engine of 1967 soul, and it's currently unrepresentable.

**Production: Layered on a soul record.** _Do Right Woman_ was assembled in pieces after the FAME session in Muscle Shoals collapsed mid-date — Aretha and Carolyn built the vocal stack in New York over an incomplete backing track. Useful proof that your Production scalar isn't a psychedelia-only field.

**Covers: 5 of 11**, plus _Do Right Woman_ from outside writers. Only four Franklin co-writes. A soul act's A&R/repertoire skill matters as much as a trad-pop act's — the Sinatra mechanic from round two generalizes forward.

## Highway 61 Revisited (Columbia, August 1965)

|#|Track|Archetype|Lyric mode|Form|Len|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|1|Like a Rolling Stone|Mid-tempo rocker|**Invective / accusation**|VerseChorus|6:13|
|2|Tombstone Blues|Stomper / rocker|**Surreal catalogue**|VerseChorus|5:58|
|3|It Takes a Lot to Laugh|Slow blues|Romantic address|TwelveBar|4:09|
|4|From a Buick 6|Shuffle / twelve-bar|Demand / boast|TwelveBar|3:19|
|5|Ballad of a Thin Man|Slow ballad|**Invective** + Character sketch|Strophic|5:58|
|6|Queen Jane Approximately|Mid-tempo rocker|Advice / exhortation|VerseChorus|5:31|
|7|Highway 61 Revisited|Shuffle / twelve-bar|**Surreal catalogue**|TwelveBar|3:30|
|8|Just Like Tom Thumb's Blues|Verse-driven song|Narrative|Strophic|5:32|
|9|Desolation Row|Verse-driven song|**Surreal catalogue**|Strophic|11:21|

**The inverse of A Hard Day's Night, and that's the headline.** Five archetypes across nine tracks — narrow, mostly rockers and 12-bars. But the Lyric mode column is the widest we've mapped. Dylan's influence came almost entirely through one axis. Put side by side:

- _A Hard Day's Night_: Archetype range 7, Lyric range **1**
- _Highway 61_: Archetype range **5**, Lyric range 6
- _Sgt. Pepper_: Archetype range 11, Lyric range 7

Three completely different artistic profiles, legible at a glance. The two-axis split from round one is fully vindicated — and it gives you a real mechanic: Dylan's 1965 impact should raise the _market value of lyric range_ industry-wide, so every folk-rock and beat act in your sim suddenly gets scored on a dimension that didn't matter in 1963.

**Last content/form bundle: rename `Story song / topical` → `Verse-driven song`.** Same fix as Waltz and Instrumental. The archetype should describe shape — many verses, refrain-only or no chorus, melody subordinate to text, high word count — while Lyric mode carries Narrative vs Topical vs Surreal catalogue. _Desolation Row_ and _El Paso_ and _Ode to Billie Joe_ share a shape and nothing else.

**New lyric modes: Invective / accusation** (_Like a Rolling Stone_, _Positively 4th Street_, _Under My Thumb_, _Like a Rolling Stone_'s whole progeny) and **Surreal catalogue** (parade of images with no narrative — _Desolation Row_, _Tombstone Blues_, later _I Am the Walrus_, _White Rabbit_). The second one is the bridge from folk-rock into psychedelia; without it, 1966–68 lyrics all collapse into "Nonsense," which is a bubblegum tag.

**Make word density a scalar, not a note.** `WordDensity 0–100` drives: Writing-stat requirement, critic score, longevity, inverse hook strength, inverse coverability, and a foreign-market penalty. Your original "high words" note on story song was right — it just belongs on every track.

**Delete my round-one "Confessional / first-person" archetype.** It duplicates the Confessional lyric mode. My error, caught by _Ballad of a Thin Man_ and the Pet Sounds mapping below.

## Pet Sounds (Capitol, May 1966)

|#|Track|Archetype|Lyric mode|Production|Mood|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|1|Wouldn't It Be Nice|Bright pop number|Romantic address|Constructed|Yearning|
|2|You Still Believe in Me|Chamber / baroque|Confessional|Constructed|Contrite|
|3|That's Not Me|Mid-tempo pop song|Confessional|Layered|Searching|
|4|Don't Talk|Reverie / mood piece|Romantic address|Constructed|Hushed|
|5|I'm Waiting for the Day|Mid-tempo pop song|Romantic address|Constructed|Impatient|
|6|Let's Go Away for Awhile|Reverie / mood piece|**Instrumental**|Constructed|Wistful|
|7|Sloop John B|Bright pop number|Narrative|Constructed|Buoyant|
|8|God Only Knows|Chamber / baroque|Romantic address|Constructed|Devotional|
|9|I Know There's an Answer|Mid-tempo pop song|Advice / exhortation|Constructed|Questioning|
|10|Here Today|Mid-tempo pop song|Advice / exhortation|Constructed|Wry|
|11|I Just Wasn't Made for These Times|Mid-tempo pop song|Confessional|Constructed|Alienated|
|12|Pet Sounds|Groove / riff vamp|**Instrumental**|Constructed|Sultry|
|13|Caroline, No|Slow ballad|Reminiscence|Constructed|Resigned|

**This album is the proof that Archetype and Production must be separate fields.** The Archetype column is almost entirely conventional — bright pop numbers, mid-tempo pop songs, two chamber pieces, a slow ballad closer, all 2:20–3:10. On form alone this looks like a competent 1964 pop LP. Everything that makes it _Pet Sounds_ lives in Production, arrangement and harmony. A model that only tracked Archetype would be blind to it.

That suggests a genuinely good hidden-information mechanic: **Archetype and Pace are visible to everyone; Production and arrangement quality are only legible to characters with high Ear/Craft.** Which is exactly what happened — Capitol didn't hear it, musicians did. It explains why the album underperformed commercially and detonated among peers, and it gives your A&R stat something real to do.

**Two instrumentals, two different archetypes.** _Let's Go Away for Awhile_ is a Reverie; the title track is a Groove/riff vamp; a Dick Dale cut would be a Stomper. All three carry Lyric mode: Instrumental. That's the round-three decision paying off cleanly.

**Rename `Mid-tempo love song` → `Mid-tempo pop song`.** Four tracks here are mid-tempo and only partly about love; _I Just Wasn't Made for These Times_ is mid-tempo and about alienation. Let Lyric mode carry the subject, same logic as "Bright pop single" → "Bright pop number." This also cleans up Revolver's _I Want to Tell You_.

**Sloop John B is a designed flaw and should be modellable as one.** Traditional arrangement, recorded a year earlier, thematically unrelated, pushed onto the album as the pre-release single. Cohesion down, sales up. If your assembly screen lets a label force a hit single onto a Unified Mood album against the producer's wishes — raising sales, lowering cohesion score and artist relationship — you've captured one of the most common real conflicts of the era.

**Two performer fields, not one.** The Wrecking Crew cut the backing tracks; the Beach Boys sang. `BackingPerformers` and `VocalPerformers`, each `Band · Session · Mixed`, drives session cost, recording speed, a quality floor independent of band skill, live reproducibility, and band morale. It also enables the very 60s move where a label quietly replaces the rhythm section.

**Vocal approach: Harmony-blend** — proposed from Aretha, confirmed here on a totally different record. Good sign the axis generalizes rather than being a soul-specific hack.

## Consolidated spec

**Archetype** (~30, single value, mutually exclusive)

_Rock/beat:_ Stomper / rocker · Mid-tempo rocker · Groove / riff vamp · Shuffle / twelve-bar · Slow blues · Jam / extended workout · Light-and-shade epic · Country two-beat / rockabilly shuffle  
_Pop:_ Bright pop number · Mid-tempo pop song · Jaunty / music-hall romp · Sing-along / chant · Dance number · Chamber / baroque piece · Dramatic ballad / big build · Slow ballad · Reverie / mood piece  
_Trad pop:_ Lush standard · Saloon ballad · Charm song · Swinger  
_Soul/R&B:_ Horn-driven soul number · Deep soul pleader · Funk workout · Spiritual / shout  
_Folk/country:_ Verse-driven song · Protest / message song  
_Art:_ Raga / modal drone · Suite / multi-part · Collage  
_Other:_ Novelty · Spoken word · Medley · Live / party record

**Lyric mode** (optional, up to 2, grouped in UI)  
_Address:_ Romantic address · Advice / exhortation · Invective / accusation · Demand / boast  
_Observation:_ Character sketch · Narrative · Scene / vignette · Reminiscence  
_Interior:_ Confessional · Topical / testimony  
_Other:_ Surreal catalogue · Nonsense · Call-and-response · Dialogue · MC patter / frame · Instrumental

**Modifiers:** Pace · Mood · Meter (4/4, 3/4, 6/8, Shuffle, Shifting) · Form (AABA, VerseChorus, TwelveBar, Strophic, ThroughComposed) · Function (Opener, Album track, Interlude, Reprise, Closer, Coda) · Production (Live in room → Overdubbed → Layered → Constructed) · VocalApproach · WordDensity · Duration · Source (Original, Cover, Traditional arrangement, Derived) · GenrePrimary + GenreSecondary · BackingPerformers / VocalPerformers

**Scout-visible by default:** Archetype, Pace, Mood, Duration, Genre. Everything else gated behind A&R/Ear/Craft stats — which is both realistic and a good reason to invest in staff.

Worth noting Archetype held at ~30 across all six albums while Lyric mode grew by three this round. That asymmetry is itself historically true: song _shapes_ were broadly stable through the decade, and what actually changed between 1960 and 1969 was what songs were allowed to be _about_. If you want one number to drive "era feel," lyric mode distribution by year is probably it.
