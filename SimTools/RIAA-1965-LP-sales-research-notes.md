# LP sales, costs, and profitability in the RIAA’s June 1965 presentation

Research notes based on inspection of the original scanned hearing pages supplied as *Copyright_Law_Revision.pdf*. Prepared November 15, 2026.

## Principal finding

In a six-company survey of **1963 releases**, including several major labels and counting subsequent **1964 sales**, **48.8% of 1,603 non-classical LP releases sold fewer than 4,000 copies**. The sample median falls in the **4,000–7,999-copy** band. The study estimated an average popular-LP break-even volume of **7,800 copies**.

These findings concern LP releases generally. **They do not establish average sales for debut LPs, or a separate average for major-label debuts.**

## 1. Industry risk and sales distributions

### Sample and measurement period — directly verified

The relevant section is titled **“Producing and Selling Records Is a Risky Business”** (pp. 797–799). Its supporting methods and data appear at pp. 861–866.

The six participating companies were:

- Mercury Records
- Capitol Records
- Columbia Records
- Laurie Records
- RCA Victor Records
- Kapp Records

Together, they accounted for approximately **32% of total industry releases in 1963** (p. 861). This is a share of releases, not a reported share of unit sales.

The survey requested data for records released in **1963**. It also counted **1964 sales of those releases**, to give late-1963 releases more time to accumulate sales (p. 861). These are therefore neither strictly first-year sales nor completed lifetime sales.

Both **monaural and stereophonic releases** are covered (p. 862). “Classical” follows each company’s catalog definition; everything else is called “non-classical” or “popular” (p. 866). “Popular” is consequently broader than the modern, narrower pop genre.

### Popular LP distribution — directly verified

The technical appendix reports the following for **1,603 popular long-playing releases** (p. 863). Percentages are reproduced as printed and reflect rounding.

| Copies sold | Number of releases | Reported percentage |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0–1,999 | 574 | 35.8% |
| 2,000–3,999 | 209 | 13.0% |
| 4,000–7,999 | 206 | 12.9% |
| 8,000–9,999 | 68 | 4.2% |
| 10,000–19,999 | 171 | 10.7% |
| 20,000–49,999 | 196 | 12.2% |
| 50,000–99,999 | 98 | 6.1% |
| 100,000–299,999 | 60 | 3.7% |
| 300,000–599,999 | 12 | 0.8% |
| 600,000–999,999 | 2 | 0.2% |
| 1,000,000 or more | 7 | 0.4% |
| **Total** | **1,603** | **100%** |

The printed cumulative percentages are **48.8% below 4,000 copies** and **61.7% below 8,000 copies**. The prose’s statement that about half sold under 4,000 is supported by the detailed table (pp. 797, 863).

**Inference from the table:** The median lies in the **4,000–7,999** band. Its exact value is unavailable. An exact arithmetic mean cannot be recovered from the grouped figures, particularly because the highest band is open-ended.

### Estimated break-even and failure rates — directly verified as study estimates

Exhibit 10 reports these figures under the existing 2-cent statutory copyright rate (p. 799):

| Format | Average break-even sales | Releases failing to earn a profit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Popular LP | 7,800 | 61% |
| Classical LP | 9,700 | 87% |
| 45 rpm single | 11,200 | 74% |

**Methodological qualification:** These rates use average break-even assumptions, rather than separate audited profit calculations for every release. The break-even averages are weighted by each company’s relative share of sampled releases (p. 865).

The **61% estimated failure rate** should be distinguished from the **61.7% observed share below 8,000 copies**. The break-even threshold was approximately 7,800, not exactly 8,000.

## 2. Costs and margins

### Survey-based per-copy model — directly verified

The appendix gives the following model for **non-classical LPs, covering mono and stereo** (p. 864):

| Item | Dollars per copy |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | $1.818 |
| Copyright license fees | $0.240 |
| Artist royalties | $0.150 |
| Music performance and other funds | $0.076 |
| Manufacturing | $0.450 |
| Selling, general and administrative | $0.450 |
| Remainder labelled “Profit Before Taxes” | **$0.452** |

**Interpretation:** The 45.2-cent remainder is not the realized net profit on every copy. The break-even method separately recovers release investment, including advances and talent costs, and allocates overhead (p. 862).

The royalty footnote says the model’s artist royalties become payable after royalties equal the relevant talent costs, sometimes including studio and related costs (p. 864). This treatment matters when interpreting the model.

The investment definition includes release-specific out-of-pocket costs such as artist advances, talent costs, and fund payments. Recording overhead and general/administrative overhead are allocated in proportion to the number of selections (p. 862).

### Effect of a proposed royalty increase — verified projections

The study models an increase from **2 cents to 3 cents per composition** by increasing copyright-license payments by 50% and holding other figures constant (p. 865):

| Format | Existing-rate break-even | Modelled break-even after 50% rate increase |
|---|---:|---:|
| Popular LP | 7,800 | 9,700 |
| Classical LP | 9,700 | 10,400 |
| 45 rpm single | 11,200 | 12,600 |

These are **projections, not observed outcomes**. The main analysis does not fully incorporate the bill’s alternative playing-time fee. For classical LPs, p. 865 separately estimates that replacing the customary quarter-cent-per-minute rate with one cent per minute would raise break-even to approximately **15,700 copies**.

### Distribution and retail pricing — verified illustration

For a **$3.98-list monaural LP**, the study illustrates an actual consumer price of approximately **$2.83** (pp. 819–821). Its model projects that passing through an additional **12 cents** in copyright fees would raise the consumer price to **$3.03**.

Page 820 gives a **$3.09** alternative if the manufacturer also preserves its existing percentage gross margin. These are modelled price effects, not documented subsequent price changes.

The distributor study underlying this part of the presentation covered **38 companies: 25 rack jobbers and 13 independent distributors** (p. 816).

## 3. Market growth versus profitability

### Sample financial results — directly verified

The financial study uses a **different, larger sample** from the six-company release survey: **19 companies for 1960–1963**, increasing to **20 in 1964**. According to p. 800, it includes all major companies. The additional 1964 company represented less than one-tenth of 1% of the sample’s 1964 sales (p. 804).

Exhibit 12 reports (p. 805):

| Sample financial result | 1960 | 1964 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Net sales | $177.3 million | $227.4 million |
| Copyright license payments | $17.4 million | $25.2 million |
| Artist royalties | $15.3 million | $21.2 million |
| After-tax profit | $6.1 million | $4.0 million |

**Calculated from the displayed figures:** Net sales increased approximately **28%**, while after-tax profit fell approximately **34%**. The study’s commentary rounds the profit decline to 33% (p. 804).

The profit series fluctuates rather than declining every year:

| Year | Sample after-tax profit |
|---|---:|
| 1960 | $6.1 million |
| 1961 | $7.1 million |
| 1962 | $10.3 million |
| 1963 | $3.4 million |
| 1964 | $4.0 million |

### Longer-run margins — directly verified

Exhibit 11 and its commentary report the following (pp. 801–803):

| Percentage of manufacturers’ net sales | 1955 | 1964 |
|---|---:|---:|
| After-tax profit | 3.6% | 1.7% |
| Copyright license fees | 8.0% | 11.1% |
| Artist royalties | 7.4% | 9.3% |
| Gross profit | 29.2% | 26.1% |

The authors attribute margin pressure partly to LP economics and mass merchandising at lower retail prices (p. 801). **That explanation is their interpretation, not an independently established causal finding.** The long series also expands its company coverage, so it is not a constant-company comparison throughout.

For the **five largest sampled companies**, each with annual sales exceeding **$10 million**, the 1964 after-tax margin was **2.9%**, compared with **1.7% for the whole sample** (p. 811).

## 4. Caveats and evidentiary limits

1. **No debut-specific results.** The release survey does not separate debut albums, established artists, or career stage. It also does not provide a major-label-only sales distribution.
2. **Not completed lifetime sales.** The survey covers 1963 releases and includes their 1964 sales. Later sales are not established by these figures.
3. **Sample scope.** The six-company sample contains several majors alongside other labels. Its 32% coverage refers to industry releases, not unit sales or revenues. A large sample is not automatically representative in every respect.
4. **Broad “popular” classification.** This means non-classical under company catalog definitions, rather than a narrowly defined pop genre (p. 866).
5. **Break-even rates are model-based.** Costs and release investment vary. The averages do not establish the precise break-even point or actual profit of a particular album (pp. 862–865).
6. **Financial figures have a broader scope than domestic LP sales.** They include domestic and export sales of records and tapes. Sales to record clubs, rather than clubs’ retail sales, enter the manufacturing accounts (p. 867). The financial sample and sales-distribution sample must not be treated as interchangeable.
7. **Sponsorship and purpose.** RIAA financed the study to argue against higher mechanical royalties. Its reported data are valuable contemporary evidence; its forecasts and causal arguments require that context.
8. **Gross receipts are not profits.** The presentation compares copyright-holder receipts with record-company profits. Representative Edwards explicitly raised this issue; Glover acknowledged that the study did not know the recipients’ operating costs (p. 891).
9. **Exact mean unavailable.** The sales bands, including the open-ended million-plus category, do not support an exact arithmetic average. A median band is defensible; a precise median is not.

## 5. Implication for early-1960s debut LP research

This source establishes that sales in the low thousands were common among a broad set of non-classical releases from a sample containing several major labels. It gives stronger distributional evidence than isolated artist examples or annual industry sales divided by new releases.

A defensible formulation is:

> A six-company survey presented to the House Judiciary Committee in June 1965 found that 48.8% of 1,603 non-classical LP releases issued in 1963 sold fewer than 4,000 copies, counting subsequent sales in 1964. The sample included Capitol, Columbia, RCA Victor, Mercury, Laurie, and Kapp. Its median release fell in the 4,000–7,999-copy band, and the study estimated an average popular-LP break-even volume of 7,800 copies. The findings do not isolate debut albums or establish a major-label debut average.

**Status:** The sales distribution, sample definitions, cost model, financial tables, and cited methodological qualifications were checked in the supplied original scan. The median band and percentage changes explicitly labelled as calculations or inferences above are derived from those figures. An average specifically for major-label debut LPs remains unverified.
